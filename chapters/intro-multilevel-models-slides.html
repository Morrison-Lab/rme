1 Correlated data

The models in earlier chapters assumed that the outcomes \(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n\) were mutually independent given the covariates. Many epidemiologic studies collect data in clusters: repeated measurements on the same person, patients treated at the same hospital, or houses in the same county (Dobson and Barnett 2018, chap. 11). Observations in the same cluster tend to resemble one another more than observations in different clusters, so the independence assumption fails. Ignoring that correlation usually makes standard errors too small, because the data carry less information than the same number of independent observations would (Dobson and Barnett 2018, chap. 11).

Two kinds of model term describe clusters. A fixed effect is a parameter for a specific group whose particular value we want to estimate, such as the effect of a specific treatment. A random effect treats the groups in the data as a sample from a larger population of groups, and models each group’s effect as a random variable drawn from a distribution; the parameters of interest are then the parameters of that distribution, especially its variance (Rocke 2021).

Example 1 (Fixed versus random effects)  

  • In a trial comparing a new drug with a placebo, the treatment groups are the only two groups of interest, so treatment is a fixed effect.
  • In a study that measures each of 20 patients several times, the 20 patients are a sample from the population of all patients, and we want conclusions that apply to patients beyond these 20, so patient is a random effect.

Random effects can be nested or crossed. Effects are nested when each lower-level group belongs to exactly one higher-level group, such as batches within laboratories. Effects are crossed when every level of one factor can appear with every level of the other, such as patients and raters when every rater scores every patient (Rocke 2021).

A model that contains both fixed and random effects is called a mixed model. The same ideas extend from linear models to logistic and Poisson regression (Rocke 2021).

2 One-way random-effects models

The simplest model with a random effect has one grouping factor and no covariates (Rocke 2021; Dobson and Barnett 2018, chap. 11).

Definition 1 (One-way random-effects model) Suppose we observe \(m\) outcomes \(Y_{i1}, \ldots, Y_{im}\) in each of \(a\) groups \(i = 1, \ldots, a\). The one-way random-effects model is \[ Y_{ij} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mu + \alpha_i + \epsilon_{ij}, \] where:

  • \(\mu\) is a fixed overall mean;
  • \(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_a \ \sim_{\operatorname{iid}}\ \operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma_\alpha^2\right)\mathclose{}\) are the random effects of the groups;
  • \(\epsilon_{ij} \ \sim_{\operatorname{iid}}\ \operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}\) are the within-group errors;
  • the \(\alpha_i\)s and the \(\epsilon_{ij}\)s are mutually independent.

The variances \(\sigma_\alpha^2\) and \(\sigma^2\) are called variance components.

Example 2 (Batches of dyestuff) The Dyestuff data in the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) record the yield of dyestuff (in grams of standard color) from \(m = 5\) preparations in each of \(a = 6\) batches of an intermediate product. The batches are a sample of all the batches the process could produce, so a one-way random-effects model treats \(\alpha_i\) as the deviation of batch \(i\)’s mean yield from the overall mean \(\mu\).

[R code]
with(Dyestuff, tapply(Yield, Batch, mean))
#>    A    B    C    D    E    F 
#> 1505 1528 1564 1498 1600 1470

The shared random effect \(\alpha_i\) is what makes observations in the same group correlated.

Theorem 1 (Moments of the one-way random-effects model) Under Definition 1, for any group \(i\) and observations \(j \neq k\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_{ij}\right]\mathclose{} &= \mu \\ \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}\right)\mathclose{} &= \sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2 \\ \operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}, Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{} &= \sigma_\alpha^2 \end{aligned} \]

and observations in different groups are independent.

Proof. This proof uses the definition of covariance, the covariance of a variable with itself, and linearity of expectation.

Mean:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_{ij}\right]\mathclose{} &= \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\mu + \alpha_i + \epsilon_{ij}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of the model)} \\ &= \mu + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\ &= \mu + 0 + 0 && \text{(both have mean zero)} \\ &= \mu && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

Covariance, for \(j\) and \(k\) equal or not:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}, Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[(Y_{ij} - \mu)(Y_{ik} - \mu)\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of covariance)} \\ &= \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[(\alpha_i + \epsilon_{ij})(\alpha_i + \epsilon_{ik})\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute the model)} \\ &= \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i^2 + \alpha_i\epsilon_{ik} + \epsilon_{ij}\alpha_i + \epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(distribute)} \\ &= \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i^2\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\alpha_i\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\ &= \sigma_\alpha^2 + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\alpha_i\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{($\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i\right]\mathclose{} = 0$, so $\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\alpha_i^2\right]\mathclose{} = \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_i\right)\mathclose{}$)} \\ &= \sigma_\alpha^2 + 0 + 0 + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(independent, mean-zero factors)} \end{aligned} \]

If \(j \neq k\), \(\epsilon_{ij}\) and \(\epsilon_{ik}\) are independent with mean zero, so \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{ik}\right]\mathclose{} = 0\) and the covariance is \(\sigma_\alpha^2\). If \(j = k\), \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\epsilon_{ij}^2\right]\mathclose{} = \sigma^2\), so the covariance of \(Y_{ij}\) with itself, which is its variance, is \(\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2\).

Observations in different groups \(i \neq i'\) are functions of the disjoint sets of independent variables \(\{\alpha_i, \epsilon_{ij}\}\) and \(\{\alpha_{i'}, \epsilon_{i'k}\}\), so they are independent.

Example 3 (Covariance between two dyestuff preparations) Suppose the batch-level variance in Example 2 is \(\sigma_\alpha^2 = 1764\) and the within-batch variance is \(\sigma^2 = 2451\). Then by Theorem 1 two preparations from the same batch would have covariance \(1764\), and each preparation would have variance \(1764 + 2451 = 4215\). Two preparations from different batches would have covariance \(0\).

Definition 2 (Intraclass correlation) Under Definition 1, the intraclass correlation is the correlation between two observations \(j \neq k\) in the same group:

\[ \rho \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}, Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}\right)\mathclose{}\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{}}} \]

Corollary 1 (Intraclass correlation in terms of variance components) Under Definition 1,

\[ \rho = \frac{\sigma_\alpha^2}{\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2}. \]

Proof. This proof uses Theorem 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}, Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}\right)\mathclose{}\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{}}} && \text{(definition of intraclass correlation)} \\ &= \frac{\sigma_\alpha^2}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}\right)\mathclose{}\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ik}\right)\mathclose{}}} && \text{(covariance from the moments theorem)} \\ &= \frac{\sigma_\alpha^2}{\sqrt{(\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2)(\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2)}} && \text{(variances from the moments theorem)} \\ &= \frac{\sigma_\alpha^2}{\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2} && \text{(square root of a square)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 4 (Intraclass correlation for the dyestuff batches) With the variance components from Example 3, Corollary 1 gives

\[ \rho = \frac{1764}{1764 + 2451} = \frac{1764}{4215} \approx 0.42, \]

so about 42% of the variation in yield is between batches.

The intraclass correlation is also the fraction of the total variance \(\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij}\right)\mathclose{} = \sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2\) that comes from the random effect.

3 Variance components

One way to estimate the variance components uses the mean squares from a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) table (Rocke 2021). Write \(\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m Y_{ij}\) for the mean of group \(i\) and \(\bar{Y}_{\cdot\cdot} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{a}\sum_{i=1}^a \bar{Y}_{i\cdot}\) for the overall mean. The between-group mean square and the within-group mean square are

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{MSA} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{m}{a-1}\sum_{i=1}^a \mathopen{}\left(\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{Y}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)\mathclose{}^2, \\ \text{MSE} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{a(m-1)}\sum_{i=1}^a\sum_{j=1}^m \mathopen{}\left(Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_{i\cdot}\right)\mathclose{}^2. \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 2 (Expected mean squares in the one-way random-effects model) Under Definition 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSE}\right]\mathclose{} &= \sigma^2 \\ \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSA}\right]\mathclose{} &= m\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]

Proof. This proof uses the fact that the sample variance of independent, identically distributed observations is unbiased for their variance, the law of iterated expectations, and the variance of a linear combination.

Within groups. Given \(\alpha_i\), the observations \(Y_{i1}, \ldots, Y_{im}\) are independent with common variance \(\sigma^2\), so their sample variance \(s_i^2 \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^m (Y_{ij} - \bar{Y}_{i\cdot})^2\) satisfies \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[s_i^2 \mid \alpha_i\right]\mathclose{} = \sigma^2\). By the law of iterated expectations, \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[s_i^2\right]\mathclose{} = \sigma^2\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSE}\right]\mathclose{} &= \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{1}{a}\sum_{i=1}^a s_i^2\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(MSE is the average of the } s_i^2\text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\sum_{i=1}^a \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[s_i^2\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\ &= \frac{1}{a}\sum_{i=1}^a \sigma^2 && \text{(each } s_i^2 \text{ is unbiased)} \\ &= \sigma^2 && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

Between groups. Each group mean is \(\bar{Y}_{i\cdot} = \mu + \alpha_i + \bar{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}\), where \(\bar{\epsilon}_{i\cdot} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m \epsilon_{ij}\). The group means are independent and identically distributed across groups, with

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\bar{Y}_{i\cdot}\right)\mathclose{} &= \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha_i\right)\mathclose{} + \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\bar{\epsilon}_{i\cdot}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(variance of a sum of independent terms)} \\ &= \sigma_\alpha^2 + \frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{j=1}^m \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\epsilon_{ij}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(variance of a linear combination)} \\ &= \sigma_\alpha^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{m} && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

\(\text{MSA}\) is \(m\) times the sample variance of \(\bar{Y}_{1\cdot}, \ldots, \bar{Y}_{a\cdot}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSA}\right]\mathclose{} &= m \cdot \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\bar{Y}_{i\cdot}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(sample variance is unbiased)} \\ &= m\mathopen{}\left(\sigma_\alpha^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{m}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= m\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2 && \text{(distribute)} \end{aligned} \]

Corollary 2 (Method-of-moments estimators of the variance components) Under Definition 1, \(\hat{\sigma}^2 \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\text{MSE}\) and \[ \hat{\sigma}_\alpha^2 \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\text{MSA} - \text{MSE}}{m} \] are unbiased estimators of \(\sigma^2\) and \(\sigma_\alpha^2\).

Proof. This proof uses Theorem 2.

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\hat{\sigma}_\alpha^2\right]\mathclose{} &= \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\text{MSA} - \text{MSE}}{m}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of the estimator)} \\ &= \frac{\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSA}\right]\mathclose{} - \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSE}\right]\mathclose{}}{m} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\ &= \frac{m\sigma_\alpha^2 + \sigma^2 - \sigma^2}{m} && \text{(expected mean squares)} \\ &= \frac{m\sigma_\alpha^2}{m} && \text{(cancel } \sigma^2\text{)} \\ &= \sigma_\alpha^2 && \text{(cancel } m\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

\(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\hat{\sigma}^2\right]\mathclose{} = \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MSE}\right]\mathclose{} = \sigma^2\) directly from Theorem 2.

Example 5 (Variance components for the dyestuff batches) The ANOVA table for Example 2 gives the mean squares:

[R code]
anova(lm(Yield ~ Batch, data = Dyestuff))

By Corollary 2, \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = 2451.3\) and \[ \hat{\sigma}_\alpha^2 = \frac{11271.5 - 2451.3}{5} = \frac{8820.2}{5} \approx 1764.0. \]

The lmer() function in the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) fits the same model by restricted maximum likelihood (REML); (1 | Batch) requests a random intercept for each batch:

[R code]
dyestuff_lmer <- lmer(Yield ~ 1 + (1 | Batch), data = Dyestuff)
VarCorr(dyestuff_lmer) |> as.data.frame()

In this balanced design with a positive moment estimate, the REML estimates match the method-of-moments estimates.

The method-of-moments estimate \(\hat{\sigma}_\alpha^2\) is negative whenever \(\text{MSA} < \text{MSE}\), which is impossible for a variance; likelihood-based methods such as REML constrain the estimate to be nonnegative, and they also handle unbalanced designs (Rocke 2021; Bates et al. 2015).

4 Nested random effects

A model can have more than one level of random effects. When each lower-level group belongs to exactly one higher-level group, the random effects are nested (Rocke 2021).

Definition 3 (Two-level nested random-effects model) Suppose \(\ell\) laboratories each analyze \(b\) batches, with \(r\) replicate measurements per batch. The two-level nested random-effects model is

\[ Y_{ijk} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mu + L_i + B_{ij} + \epsilon_{ijk}, \]

where \(L_i \ \sim_{\operatorname{iid}}\ \operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma_L^2\right)\mathclose{}\) is the effect of laboratory \(i\), \(B_{ij} \ \sim_{\operatorname{iid}}\ \operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma_B^2\right)\mathclose{}\) is the effect of batch \(j\) within laboratory \(i\), \(\epsilon_{ijk} \ \sim_{\operatorname{iid}}\ \operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}\) is the replicate error, and all three sets of random variables are mutually independent.

By the same argument as Theorem 2, the expected mean squares of the nested ANOVA are \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MS}_L\right]\mathclose{} = br\sigma_L^2 + r\sigma_B^2 + \sigma^2\), \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MS}_B\right]\mathclose{} = r\sigma_B^2 + \sigma^2\), and \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\text{MS}_E\right]\mathclose{} = \sigma^2\) (Rocke 2021). So the correct \(F\) test for laboratories divides \(\text{MS}_L\) by \(\text{MS}_B\), not by \(\text{MS}_E\).

Example 6 (A cooperative trial) The coop data in the MASS package (Venables and Ripley 2002) come from a cooperative trial in which \(\ell = 6\) laboratories each analyzed \(b = 3\) batches, a month apart, with \(r = 2\) replicates per batch. We use the measurements of specimen S1:

[R code]
coop2 <- subset(MASS::coop, Spc == "S1")
anova(lm(Conc ~ Lab + Lab:Bat, data = coop2))

The Lab:Bat term is the batch-within-laboratory effect. The \(F\) statistic that anova() prints for Lab uses \(\text{MS}_E\) as its denominator, which is wrong here; the correct statistic is \(F = 0.37804 / 0.01703 \approx 22.2\) on \((5, 12)\) degrees of freedom.

The equivalent mixed model has a random intercept for each laboratory and for each batch within each laboratory:

[R code]
coop_lmer <- lmer(Conc ~ 1 + (1 | Lab) + (1 | Lab:Bat), data = coop2)
VarCorr(coop_lmer) |> as.data.frame()

The estimated variance components are \(\hat{\sigma}^2 \approx 0.0063\) (same-day replicates), \(\hat{\sigma}_B^2 \approx 0.0054\) (batches a month apart), and \(\hat{\sigma}_L^2 \approx 0.0602\) (laboratories). Most of the variation in the measurements comes from differences between laboratories.

Testing whether a variance component is zero is harder than testing a fixed effect, because \(\sigma^2 = 0\) lies on the boundary of the parameter space: a variance cannot be negative, so the usual large-sample distribution of the likelihood ratio statistic does not apply (Rocke 2021).

5 Multilevel models: complete, no, and partial pooling

A multilevel model is a regression model whose coefficients are allowed to vary by group, with the group-level coefficients themselves given a model (Gelman and Hill 2007, chap. 12). The random-effects models in Definition 1 and Definition 3 are the simplest multilevel models.

Example 7 (Radon in Minnesota homes) Gelman and Hill (2007, chap. 12) analyze measurements of indoor radon, a radioactive gas that can cause lung cancer, from 919 houses in 85 Minnesota counties. We use the copy of these data distributed with the rstanarm R package (Goodrich et al. 2024):

  • log_radon: natural log of the radon measurement in the house;
  • floor: 0 if the measurement was taken in the basement, 1 if on the first floor;
  • county: the county the house is in;
  • log_uranium: natural log of the county-level soil uranium measurement.
[R code]
load(here::here("inst/extdata/radon.rda"))
radon_n <- table(radon$county)
summary(as.vector(radon_n))
#>    Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max. 
#>     1.0     3.0     5.0    10.8    10.0   116.0

The number of houses per county varies from 1 to 116.

There are three ways to estimate the mean log radon level in each county (Gelman and Hill 2007, chap. 12):

  • Complete pooling ignores the counties and estimates every county’s mean by the overall mean.
  • No pooling estimates each county’s mean by its own sample mean \(\bar{y}_j\), which is very noisy for counties with only one or two houses.
  • Partial pooling fits the random-intercept model \(Y_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_j + \epsilon_{ij}\) for house \(i\) in county \(j\) (Definition 1, with the roles of the indices swapped and unequal group sizes \(n_j\)) and estimates each county’s mean as a compromise between \(\bar{y}_j\) and \(\mu\).

Theorem 3 (Partial-pooling estimate of a group mean) In the random-intercept model with known \(\mu\), \(\sigma_\alpha^2\), and \(\sigma^2\), the predicted mean of group \(j\), \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\mu + \alpha_j \mid \text{data}\right]\mathclose{}\), is

\[ \hat{\mu}_j = w_j\bar{y}_j + (1 - w_j)\mu, \qquad w_j \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{n_j / \sigma^2}{n_j / \sigma^2 + 1 / \sigma_\alpha^2}, \]

a weighted average of the group’s sample mean and the overall mean, with weights proportional to their precisions (Gelman and Hill 2007, chap. 12).

The proof of Theorem 3 uses the conditional distribution of one normal variable given another, which is beyond the scope of this chapter. The weight \(w_j\) increases with the group size \(n_j\): large groups keep estimates close to their own sample means, and small groups are pulled, or shrunk, toward the overall mean.

Example 8 (Shrinkage of county mean radon levels) We fit the random-intercept model to the radon data from Example 7:

[R code]
radon_lmer0 <- lmer(log_radon ~ 1 + (1 | county), data = radon)
VarCorr(radon_lmer0) |> as.data.frame()
[R code]
fixef(radon_lmer0)
#> (Intercept) 
#>     1.34983

The estimates are \(\hat{\mu }\approx 1.350\), \(\hat{\sigma}_\alpha^2 \approx 0.0886\), and \(\hat{\sigma}^2 \approx 0.587\). Aitkin County has \(n_j = 4\) houses with sample mean \(\bar{y}_j \approx 0.715\). Plugging these estimates into Theorem 3:

\[ \begin{aligned} w_j &= \frac{4 / 0.587}{4 / 0.587 + 1 / 0.0886} && \text{(substitute the estimates)} \\ &\approx \frac{6.81}{6.81 + 11.29} && \text{(divide)} \\ &\approx 0.377 && \text{(divide)} \\ \hat{\mu}_j &\approx 0.377 \times 0.715 + 0.623 \times 1.350 && \text{(substitute into the weighted average)} \\ &\approx 1.111 && \text{(multiply and add)} \end{aligned} \]

Aitkin County’s estimate moves most of the way from its own mean toward the overall mean. In contrast, Hennepin County has \(n_j = 105\) houses, so its weight is about 0.94 and its estimate barely moves. The same calculation for every county reproduces the estimates that lme4 reports:

[R code]
radon_vc <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(radon_lmer0))$vcov
radon_w <- (radon_n / radon_vc[2]) /
  (radon_n / radon_vc[2] + 1 / radon_vc[1])
radon_ybar <- tapply(radon$log_radon, radon$county, mean)
radon_pp <- radon_w * radon_ybar + (1 - radon_w) * fixef(radon_lmer0)
max(abs(radon_pp - coef(radon_lmer0)$county[, 1]))
#> [1] 3.77476e-15
[R code]
plot(
  as.vector(radon_n), radon_ybar,
  log = "x", pch = 1,
  xlab = "Houses sampled in county (log scale)",
  ylab = "Estimated county mean log radon"
)
points(as.vector(radon_n), radon_pp, pch = 19)

6 Adding predictors to a multilevel model

Covariates enter a multilevel model as fixed effects, and their coefficients can also vary by group (Gelman and Hill 2007, chap. 12). For house \(i\) in county \(j\) with floor indicator \(x_{ij}\):

  • the varying-intercept model is \(Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \alpha_j + \beta_1 x_{ij} + \epsilon_{ij}\);
  • the varying-intercept, varying-slope model is \(Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \alpha_j + (\beta_1 + \gamma_j) x_{ij} + \epsilon_{ij}\), where each county’s pair \((\alpha_j, \gamma_j)\) is bivariate normal with mean zero.

Example 9 (Radon by floor of measurement)  

[R code]
radon_lmer1 <- lmer(log_radon ~ floor + (1 | county), data = radon)
radon_lmer2 <- lmer(log_radon ~ floor + (1 + floor | county), data = radon)
fixef(radon_lmer1)
#> (Intercept)       floor 
#>    1.492393   -0.662889
VarCorr(radon_lmer2)
#>  Groups   Name        Std.Dev. Corr  
#>  county   (Intercept) 0.3371         
#>           floor       0.3285   -0.37 
#>  Residual             0.7168

In the varying-intercept model, measurements on the first floor average about 0.66 lower on the log scale than basement measurements in the same county, a ratio of \(\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{(\right\}\mathclose{}-0.66) \approx 0.52\). The varying-slope model lets that difference vary by county.

To compare the two models, anova() refits both by maximum likelihood and computes a likelihood ratio test:

[R code]
anova(radon_lmer1, radon_lmer2)

The varying-slope model adds two parameters (the slope variance and its correlation with the intercept), and the likelihood ratio test gives \(p \approx 0.26\). Because the null value of the slope variance, zero, is on the boundary of the parameter space, this \(p\)-value is conservative (Rocke 2021); AIC and BIC also favor the simpler model.

Example 10 (A county-level predictor) Soil uranium is measured once per county, so it is a group-level predictor. Adding it to the varying-intercept model:

[R code]
radon_lmer3 <- lmer(
  log_radon ~ floor + log_uranium + (1 | county),
  data = radon
)
fixef(radon_lmer3)
#> (Intercept)       floor log_uranium 
#>    1.496157   -0.638778    0.697499
VarCorr(radon_lmer3) |> as.data.frame()

The estimated between-county variance falls from about 0.099 without uranium to about 0.022 with it, while the within-county variance barely changes: county uranium explains most of the differences between counties.

7 Multilevel logistic regression

Random effects can be added to any generalized linear model. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) adds random effects to the linear predictor, so for a binary outcome with random intercepts,

\[ \operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\frac{\pi_{ij}}{1 - \pi_{ij}}\right\}\mathclose{} = {\tilde{x}_{ij}}^{\top}\tilde{\beta}+ \alpha_j, \qquad \alpha_j \ \sim_{\operatorname{iid}}\ \operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, \sigma_\alpha^2\right)\mathclose{} \]

(Dobson and Barnett 2018, chap. 11; Gelman and Hill 2007, chap. 14). Each group’s odds are multiplied by \(\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\alpha_j\right\}\mathclose{}\), so about 95% of groups have odds within a factor of \(\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\pm 2\sigma_\alpha\right\}\mathclose{}\) of the odds for a typical group (Rocke 2021).

Example 11 (Contagious bovine pleuropneumonia) The cbpp data in the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) record the number of new cases of contagious bovine pleuropneumonia in 15 herds of zebu cattle in Ethiopia, each observed in up to four periods. The herds are a sample of all herds, so we model herd as a random effect and period as a fixed effect:

[R code]
cbpp_glmer <- glmer(
  cbind(incidence, size - incidence) ~ period + (1 | herd),
  family = binomial,
  data = cbpp
)
summary(cbpp_glmer)$coefficients
#>              Estimate Std. Error  z value    Pr(>|z|)
#> (Intercept) -1.398343   0.231214 -6.04783 1.46810e-09
#> period2     -0.991925   0.303151 -3.27205 1.06769e-03
#> period3     -1.128216   0.322830 -3.49477 4.74473e-04
#> period4     -1.579745   0.422049 -3.74304 1.81808e-04
VarCorr(cbpp_glmer)
#>  Groups Name        Std.Dev.
#>  herd   (Intercept) 0.6421

The estimated herd standard deviation is \(\hat{\sigma}_\alpha \approx 0.642\), so about 95% of herds have odds of a new case between \(\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-2 \times 0.642\right\}\mathclose{} \approx 0.28\) and \(\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{2 \times 0.642\right\}\mathclose{} \approx 3.6\) times those of a typical herd. Holding herd fixed, the odds of a new case in period 4 are \(\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-1.58\right\}\mathclose{} \approx 0.21\) times the odds in period 1.

The fixed-effect coefficients in a GLMM are conditional on the random effect: they compare odds within the same group. Because the logit link is nonlinear, these coefficients are not the same as population-averaged odds ratios (Dobson and Barnett 2018, chap. 11).
