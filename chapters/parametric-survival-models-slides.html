1.1 Exponential Distribution

The exponential distribution is the baseline parametric model in survival analysis. It assumes a constant hazard rate \(\lambda > 0\) over time. This constant hazard implies that the event probability in any short interval depends only on the interval duration and not on elapsed time (the memoryless property).

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{f}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\lambda \text{e}^{-\lambda t} && \text{(probability density function definition for $t \ge 0$)} \\ {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\operatorname{f}(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} &= {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}} - \lambda t && \text{(taking natural logarithm of density)} \\ \operatorname{F}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{t} \operatorname{f}(u) du && \text{(cumulative distribution function definition)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{t} \lambda \text{e}^{-\lambda u} du && \text{(substituting probability density function)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ -\text{e}^{-\lambda u} \right]\mathclose{}_{0}^{t} && \text{(evaluating antiderivative)} \\ &= 1 - \text{e}^{-\lambda t} && \text{(evaluating limits of integration)} \\ \operatorname{S}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}1 - \operatorname{F}(t) && \text{(survival function definition)} \\ &= 1 - \mathopen{}\left(1 - \text{e}^{-\lambda t}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substituting cumulative distribution function)} \\ &= \text{e}^{-\lambda t} && \text{(simplifying terms)} \\ {\Lambda}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\operatorname{S}(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(cumulative hazard function definition)} \\ &= -{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\text{e}^{-\lambda t}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting survival function)} \\ &= \lambda t && \text{(simplifying logarithm of exponential)} \\ {\lambda}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} {\Lambda}(t) && \text{(hazard function definition as derivative of cumulative hazard)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\lambda t) && \text{(substituting cumulative hazard)} \\ &= \lambda && \text{(differentiating linear term)} \\ {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T\right]\mathclose{}} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{\infty} \operatorname{S}(t) dt && \text{(expectation formula for non-negative continuous random variable)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \text{e}^{-\lambda t} dt && \text{(substituting survival function)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ -\frac{1}{\lambda} \text{e}^{-\lambda t} \right]\mathclose{}_{0}^{\infty} && \text{(evaluating antiderivative)} \\ &= \lambda^{-1} && \text{(evaluating limits of integration)} \end{aligned} \]

Theorem 1 (The exponential distribution is memoryless) If \(T\) has an exponential distribution with rate \(\lambda > 0\), then for all \(s \ge 0\) and \(t \ge 0\),

\[ \mathbb{P}(T > s + t \mid T > s) = \mathbb{P}(T > t). \]

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(T > s + t \mid T > s) &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(T > s + t,\ T > s)}{\mathbb{P}(T > s)} && \text{(definition of conditional probability)} \\ &= \frac{\mathbb{P}(T > s + t)}{\mathbb{P}(T > s)} && \text{(the event } T > s + t \text{ implies } T > s \text{)} \\ &= \frac{\operatorname{S}(s + t)}{\operatorname{S}(s)} && \text{(definition of the survival function)} \\ &= \frac{\text{e}^{-\lambda (s + t)}}{\text{e}^{-\lambda s}} && \text{(exponential survival function)} \\ &= \text{e}^{-\lambda (s + t) + \lambda s} && \text{(quotient rule for exponentials)} \\ &= \text{e}^{-\lambda t} && \text{(cancel } \lambda s \text{)} \\ &= \mathbb{P}(T > t) && \text{(exponential survival function)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 1 (Remaining survival after 10 event-free weeks) Let \(T\) be exponential with rate \(\lambda = 0.1\) events per week. For a subject who has been event-free for \(s = 10\) weeks, the probability of lasting at least \(t = 5\) more weeks is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(T > 15 \mid T > 10) &= \frac{\text{e}^{-0.1 \cdot 15}}{\text{e}^{-0.1 \cdot 10}} && \text{(ratio of survival functions)} \\ &= \frac{\text{e}^{-1.5}}{\text{e}^{-1}} && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= \text{e}^{-0.5} && \text{(quotient rule for exponentials)} \\ &\approx 0.6065 && \text{(evaluate)} \end{aligned} \]

This value equals \(\mathbb{P}(T > 5) = \text{e}^{-0.1 \cdot 5} = \text{e}^{-0.5}\), the probability that a new subject lasts 5 weeks: the 10 weeks already survived do not change the outlook.

Example 2 (A Weibull distribution with shape 2 is not memoryless (counterexample)) Now let \(T\) be Weibull with \(\lambda = 0.1\) and \(p = 2\), so \(\operatorname{S}(t) = \text{e}^{-(0.1 t)^2}\) and the hazard increases over time. Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(T > 15 \mid T > 10) &= \frac{\text{e}^{-(0.1 \cdot 15)^2}}{\text{e}^{-(0.1 \cdot 10)^2}} && \text{(ratio of survival functions)} \\ &= \frac{\text{e}^{-(1.5)^2}}{\text{e}^{-(1)^2}} && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= \frac{\text{e}^{-2.25}}{\text{e}^{-1}} && \text{(square)} \\ &= \text{e}^{-1.25} && \text{(quotient rule for exponentials)} \\ &\approx 0.2865 && \text{(evaluate)} \end{aligned} \]

whereas \(\mathbb{P}(T > 5) = \text{e}^{-(0.5)^2} = \text{e}^{-0.25} \approx 0.7788\). A subject who has already lasted 10 weeks is less likely to last 5 more weeks than a new subject is, because the hazard has risen in the meantime.

1.2 Weibull Distribution

The Weibull distribution generalizes the exponential distribution by introducing a shape parameter \(p > 0\) alongside the scale parameter \(\lambda > 0\) [2]. This flexibility allows the hazard rate to change monotonically over time, accommodating situations where risk increases or decreases as time elapses.

Using the parameterization of Kalbfleisch and Prentice [2]:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{f}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\lambda p (\lambda t)^{p-1} \text{e}^{-(\lambda t)^p} && \text{(probability density function for $t \ge 0$)} \\ \operatorname{F}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{t} \operatorname{f}(u) du && \text{(cumulative distribution function definition)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{t} \lambda p (\lambda u)^{p-1} \text{e}^{-(\lambda u)^p} du && \text{(substituting density function)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ -\text{e}^{-(\lambda u)^p} \right]\mathclose{}_{0}^{t} && \text{(evaluating antiderivative via substitution $w = (\lambda u)^p$)} \\ &= 1 - \text{e}^{-(\lambda t)^p} && \text{(evaluating limits of integration)} \\ \operatorname{S}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}1 - \operatorname{F}(t) && \text{(survival function definition)} \\ &= 1 - \mathopen{}\left(1 - \text{e}^{-(\lambda t)^p}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substituting cumulative distribution function)} \\ &= \text{e}^{-(\lambda t)^p} && \text{(simplifying terms)} \\ {\Lambda}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\operatorname{S}(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(cumulative hazard function definition)} \\ &= -{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\text{e}^{-(\lambda t)^p}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting survival function)} \\ &= (\lambda t)^p && \text{(simplifying logarithm of exponential)} \\ {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\Lambda}(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} &= {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{(\lambda t)^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(taking natural logarithm of cumulative hazard)} \\ &= p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda t\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(applying exponent rule for logarithms)} \\ &= p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}} + p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{t\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(applying product rule for logarithms)} \\ {\lambda}(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} {\Lambda}(t) && \text{(hazard function definition)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathopen{}\left( (\lambda t)^p \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substituting cumulative hazard)} \\ &= p (\lambda t)^{p-1} \cdot \lambda && \text{(applying power and chain rules of differentiation)} \\ &= \lambda p (\lambda t)^{p-1} && \text{(rearranging factors)} \\ {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T\right]\mathclose{}} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{\infty} \operatorname{S}(t) dt && \text{(expectation formula for non-negative random variable)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \text{e}^{-(\lambda t)^p} dt && \text{(substituting survival function)} \\ &= \frac{1}{\lambda p} \int_{0}^{\infty} u^{\frac{1}{p}-1} \text{e}^{-u} du && \text{(substituting $u = (\lambda t)^p \implies t = \lambda^{-1} u^{1/p}$)} \\ &= \lambda^{-1} \cdot \Gamma\mathopen{}\left(1 + \frac{1}{p}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(applying definition of Gamma function $\Gamma(z)$)} \end{aligned} \]

Note

Recall from calculus:

  • \(\Gamma(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{u=0}^{\infty}u^{t-1}\text{e}^{-u}du\)

  • \(\Gamma(t) = (t-1)!\) for integers \(t \in \mathbb Z\)

  • It is implemented by the gamma() function in R.

Properties of Weibull hazard functions

Theorem 2 (Properties of Weibull hazard functions) If \(T\) has a Weibull distribution, then:

  • When \(p=1\), the Weibull distribution simplifies to the exponential distribution.
  • When \(p > 1\), the hazard is strictly increasing: \({\lambda}'(t) > 0\).
  • When \(p < 1\), the hazard is strictly decreasing: \({\lambda}'(t) < 0\).
  • \(\log{{\Lambda}(t)}\) is a straight line relative to \(\log{t}\): \(\log{{\Lambda}(t)} = p \log{\lambda} + p \log{t}\).

Proof. We prove each property of the Weibull hazard function \({\lambda}(t) = \lambda p (\lambda t)^{p-1}\):

  1. Simplification to Exponential distribution when \(p=1\): Setting \(p=1\) in the Weibull hazard function yields: \[ \begin{aligned} {\lambda}(t) &= \lambda (1) (\lambda t)^{1-1} && \text{(substituting $p=1$ into Weibull hazard formula)} \\ &= \lambda \cdot 1 \cdot (\lambda t)^0 && \text{(simplifying exponent $1-1 = 0$)} \\ &= \lambda && \text{(since $(\lambda t)^0 = 1$ for $t > 0$)} \end{aligned} \] This constant value is the hazard function of the exponential distribution with parameter \(\lambda\).

  2. Increasing hazard when \(p > 1\): Differentiating \({\lambda}(t) = \lambda^p p t^{p-1}\) with respect to \(t\) gives: \[ \begin{aligned} {\lambda}'(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathopen{}\left( \lambda^p p t^{p-1} \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of hazard derivative)} \\ &= \lambda^p p (p-1) t^{p-2} && \text{(applying power rule of differentiation)} \end{aligned} \] Since \(\lambda > 0\), \(p > 0\), and \(t > 0\), the sign of \({\lambda}'(t)\) depends entirely on the factor \((p-1)\). When \(p > 1\), \((p-1) > 0\), so \({\lambda}'(t) > 0\) for all \(t > 0\), meaning the hazard function is strictly increasing over time.

  3. Decreasing hazard when \(p < 1\): Using the derivative \({\lambda}'(t) = \lambda^p p (p-1) t^{p-2}\): When \(p < 1\), \((p-1) < 0\), so \({\lambda}'(t) < 0\) for all \(t > 0\), meaning the hazard function is strictly decreasing over time.

  4. Linear relationship between \(\log {\Lambda}(t)\) and \(\log t\): Taking the natural logarithm of the cumulative hazard function \({\Lambda}(t) = (\lambda t)^p\): \[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\Lambda}(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{(\lambda t)^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting Weibull cumulative hazard)} \\ &= p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda t\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(applying logarithm exponent rule $\log(a^b) = b \log a$)} \\ &= p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}} + p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{t\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(applying logarithm product rule $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$)} \end{aligned} \] Defining \(y \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\Lambda}(t)\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and \(x \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{t\right\}\mathclose{}}\), this equation takes the linear form \(y = a + b x\) with intercept \(a = p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and slope \(b = p\).

Example 3 (Evaluating Weibull hazard shapes under different shape parameters) Consider a clinical trial evaluating time to relapse in months following cancer treatment, modeled using a Weibull distribution with scale parameter \(\lambda = 0.1\). We evaluate the hazard rate \({\lambda}(t)\) at months \(t = 1, 6, 12\) under three distinct shape parameters:

  1. Decreasing hazard (\(p = 0.5\)): The hazard rate formula gives: \[ {\lambda}(t) = (0.1)(0.5)(0.1 t)^{-0.5} = \frac{0.05}{\sqrt{0.1 t}} \]

    • At \(t = 1\) month: \({\lambda}(1) = \frac{0.05}{\sqrt{0.1}} \approx 0.1581\) events per month.
    • At \(t = 6\) months: \({\lambda}(6) = \frac{0.05}{\sqrt{0.6}} \approx 0.0645\) events per month.
    • At \(t = 12\) months: \({\lambda}(12) = \frac{0.05}{\sqrt{1.2}} \approx 0.0456\) events per month. The risk of relapse is highest immediately following treatment and declines over time.

  2. Constant hazard (\(p = 1.0\)): The hazard rate formula reduces to \({\lambda}(t) = \lambda = 0.1000\) events per month for all \(t\). The risk of relapse remains constant regardless of time elapsed.

  3. Increasing hazard (\(p = 2.0\)): The hazard rate formula gives: \[ {\lambda}(t) = (0.1)(2.0)(0.1 t)^{1.0} = 0.02 t \]

    • At \(t = 1\) month: \({\lambda}(1) = 0.02(1) = 0.0200\) events per month.
    • At \(t = 6\) months: \({\lambda}(6) = 0.02(6) = 0.1200\) events per month.
    • At \(t = 12\) months: \({\lambda}(12) = 0.02(12) = 0.2400\) events per month. The risk of relapse increases linearly over time.

1.3 Exponential Regression

Definition 1 (Exponential regression model) An exponential regression model for an event time \(T\) with predictor vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) assumes that, given \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\), \(T\) has an exponential distribution whose hazard rate is the exponentiated linear predictor:

\[ {\lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}}, \quad \eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}= \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p \tag{1}\]

Example 4 (Exponential regression with one binary covariate) Let \(x_1 = 1\) for patients on a standard treatment and \(x_1 = 0\) for patients on a new treatment, and suppose \(\beta_0 = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{0.025\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and \(\beta_1 = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{4\right\}\mathclose{}}\), with time measured in weeks. By Equation 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\lambda}(t \mid x_1 = 0) &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_0\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(exponential regression hazard with } x_1 = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 0.025 && \text{(} {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{0.025\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} = 0.025 \text{)} \\ {\lambda}(t \mid x_1 = 1) &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_0 + \beta_1\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(exponential regression hazard with } x_1 = 1 \text{)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_0\right\}\mathclose{}} \cdot {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_1\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(product rule for exponentials)} \\ &= 0.025 \cdot 4 && \text{(evaluate each factor)} \\ &= 0.1 && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

Each group’s mean event time is the reciprocal of its hazard rate, as derived in Section 1.1: \(1/0.025 = 40\) weeks on the new treatment and \(1/0.1 = 10\) weeks on the standard treatment.

Exponential regression models the hazard rate using a log link function. At \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), Equation 1 gives the baseline hazard rate:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\lambda}_0(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}) && \text{(baseline hazard rate definition at $\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}$)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting $\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}$ into hazard function)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_0 + (\beta_1 \cdot 0 + \dots + \beta_p \cdot 0)\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(evaluating linear predictor at zero vector)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_0\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(simplifying exponent)} \end{aligned} \]

When there are no additional predictors (\(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\)), the baseline hazard is constant: \(\lambda = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_0\right\}\mathclose{}}\). This model uses a log link as in a generalized linear model for Poisson or rate outcomes. Because the hazard rate does not depend on time \(t\), the hazard ratio comparing two covariate vectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and \(\mathord{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*}}\) is constant over time:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{ϙ}_{{\lambda}}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}: \mathord{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*}}) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{{\lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}{{\lambda}(t \mid \mathord{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*}})} && \text{(hazard ratio definition)} \\ &= \frac{{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}}}{{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*}})\right\}\mathclose{}}} && \text{(substituting exponential regression hazard rates)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) - \eta(\mathord{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*}})\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(applying quotient rule for exponentials)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{{\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}^*}}) \cdot \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\beta}}\vphantom{\beta}}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(expressing difference in linear predictors)} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, exponential regression models possess proportional hazards trivially.

1.4 Accelerated Failure Time

In a proportional hazards (PH) model, covariates act multiplicatively on the baseline hazard function:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\lambda}_0(t) \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(proportional hazards hazard rate definition)} \\ {\Lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{t} {\lambda}(u \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) du && \text{(cumulative hazard function definition)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{t} {\lambda}_0(u) \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) du && \text{(substituting proportional hazard rate)} \\ &= \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \cdot {\Lambda}_0(t) && \text{(factoring constant hazard multiplier out of integral)} \\ \operatorname{S}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-{\Lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(survival function in terms of cumulative hazard)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \cdot {\Lambda}_0(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting cumulative hazard)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-{\Lambda}_0(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} \right]\mathclose{}^{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})} && \text{(applying exponent laws)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ \operatorname{S}_0(t) \right]\mathclose{}^{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})} && \text{(substituting baseline survival function $\operatorname{S}_0(t)$)} \end{aligned} \]

An alternative modeling framework is the Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) model. Rather than multiplying the hazard rate, covariates accelerate or decelerate the progression of time itself.

Definition 2 (Acceleration factor) For a covariate vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) and coefficients \(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_p\), let \(\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_p x_p\), with no intercept. The acceleration factor is

\[ \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}}. \]

Example 5 (Computing an acceleration factor) Suppose there is one covariate, with coefficient \(\beta_1 = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}}\), and a subject has \(x_1 = 2\). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of the acceleration factor)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\beta_1 x_1\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(one covariate, no intercept)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{2 {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substitute } \beta_1 = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}} \text{ and } x_1 = 2 \text{)} \\ &= 4 && \text{(} {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{a\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} = a \text{, and } 2 {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{4\right\}\mathclose{}} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Definition 3 (Accelerated failure time model) An accelerated failure time (AFT) model for an event time \(T\) with covariate vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) assumes that

\[ \operatorname{S}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\operatorname{S}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} \tag{2}\]

where:

  • \(\operatorname{S}_0(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{P}(T > t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}})\) is the baseline survival function, and
  • \(\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) is the acceleration factor (Definition 2).

An acceleration factor \(\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) > 1\) makes time pass faster for a subject with covariates \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) than at baseline, so events happen sooner; \(\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) < 1\) slows time down, so events happen later.

Example 6 (An acceleration factor of 4) Suppose the baseline survival function is exponential with mean 40 weeks, \(\operatorname{S}_0(t) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-t/40\right\}\mathclose{}}\), and a covariate pattern \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) has acceleration factor \(\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 4\). By Equation 2, the probability that a subject with covariates \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is still event-free at 10 weeks is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{S}(10 \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \operatorname{S}_0(10 \cdot 4) && \text{(AFT survival function with } t = 10 \text{ and } \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 4 \text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{S}_0(40) && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-40/40\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substitute the baseline survival function)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-1\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(divide)} \\ &\approx 0.368 && \text{(evaluate)} \end{aligned} \]

A subject with covariates \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) has the same chance of lasting 10 weeks that a baseline subject has of lasting 40 weeks.

Theorem 3 (AFT models rescale every quantile) In an AFT model (Definition 3), suppose the baseline survival function \(\operatorname{S}_0\) is continuous and strictly decreasing, and let \(t_q^0\) be the baseline time by which a fraction \(q\) of subjects have had the event, so that \(\operatorname{S}_0(t_q^0) = 1 - q\). Then the corresponding time for covariate pattern \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is

\[ t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \frac{t_q^0}{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}. \]

Proof. The time \(t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) solves \(\operatorname{S}(t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 1 - q\):

\[ \begin{aligned} 1 - q &= \operatorname{S}(t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(definition of } t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \text{)} \\ &= \operatorname{S}_0\mathopen{}\left(t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(AFT survival function)} \end{aligned} \]

Because \(\operatorname{S}_0\) is strictly decreasing, \(t_q^0\) is the only time at which \(\operatorname{S}_0\) equals \(1 - q\), so:

\[ \begin{aligned} t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= t_q^0 && \text{(} \operatorname{S}_0 \text{ is one-to-one)} \\ t_q(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{t_q^0}{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})} && \text{(divide by } \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) > 0 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 7 (Median event times with an acceleration factor of 4) In Example 6, the baseline survival function is \(\operatorname{S}_0(t) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-t/40\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Its median (\(q = 0.5\)) solves \(\operatorname{S}_0(t_{0.5}^0) = 0.5\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-t_{0.5}^0 / 40\right\}\mathclose{}} &= 0.5 && \text{(definition of the median)} \\ -t_{0.5}^0 / 40 &= {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{0.5\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(take logs)} \\ t_{0.5}^0 &= -40 {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{0.5\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(multiply by } -40 \text{)} \\ &= 40 {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(} -{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{0.5\right\}\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{1/0.5\right\}\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}} \text{)} \\ &\approx 27.73 && \text{(evaluate)} \end{aligned} \]

By Theorem 3, with \(\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 4\), the median for covariate pattern \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) is \(27.73 / 4 \approx 6.93\) weeks. To check: \(\operatorname{S}(6.93 \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \operatorname{S}_0(6.93 \cdot 4) = \operatorname{S}_0(27.73) = 0.5\).

We derive the expected survival time under the AFT model using change-of-variables integration:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\right]\mathclose{}} &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{\infty} \operatorname{S}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) dt && \text{(expectation formula for non-negative random variable)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \operatorname{S}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} dt && \text{(substituting AFT survival function)} \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \operatorname{S}_0(u) \cdot \frac{du}{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})} && \text{(substituting $u = t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \implies dt = \frac{du}{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})}$)} \\ &= \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^{-1} \cdot \int_{0}^{\infty} \operatorname{S}_0(u) du && \text{(factoring constant $\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^{-1}$ out of integral)} \\ &= \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^{-1} \cdot {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\right]\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting baseline expectation formula)} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, the mean survival time for a subject with covariates \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) equals the baseline mean survival time divided by the acceleration factor \(\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}}\).

We also derive the cumulative hazard and hazard rate functions under the AFT formulation:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\Lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\operatorname{S}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(cumulative hazard function definition)} \\ &= -{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\operatorname{S}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting AFT survival function)} \\ &= {\Lambda}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substituting baseline cumulative hazard)} \\ {\lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial t} {\Lambda}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(hazard function definition)} \\ &= \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathopen{}\left( {\Lambda}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(substituting AFT cumulative hazard)} \\ &= {\Lambda}_0'\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(applying chain rule of differentiation)} \\ &= {\lambda}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) && \text{(since ${\Lambda}_0'(u) = {\lambda}_0(u)$)} \\ &= \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \cdot {\lambda}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(rearranging factors)} \end{aligned} \]

Relationship between PH and AFT models

If the baseline distribution is exponential with parameter \(\lambda\), substituting \(\operatorname{S}_0(t) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\lambda t\right\}\mathclose{}}\) into the AFT model yields:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{S}(t \mid \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\lambda \cdot t \cdot \theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substituting exponential baseline into AFT formula)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\lambda t\right\}\mathclose{}} \right]\mathclose{}^{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})} && \text{(applying exponent laws)} \\ &= \mathopen{}\left[ \operatorname{S}_0(t) \right]\mathclose{}^{\theta_{{\lambda}}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})} && \text{(substituting exponential baseline survival function)} \end{aligned} \]

This derivation demonstrates that the exponential AFT model is mathematically identical to the exponential PH model.

The Weibull distribution has the same property.

Theorem 4 (A Weibull proportional hazards model is an AFT model) Let \(\operatorname{S}_0(t) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-(\lambda t)^p\right\}\mathclose{}}\) be a Weibull baseline survival function with scale \(\lambda > 0\) and shape \(p > 0\). If subject \(i\) has hazard \({\lambda}_0(t) \cdot \theta_i\) for a constant \(\theta_i > 0\), then subject \(i\)’s survival function is

\[ \operatorname{S}_i(t) = \operatorname{S}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_i^{1/p} \right)\mathclose{}, \]

so the model is also an accelerated failure time model, with acceleration factor \(\theta_i^{1/p}\).

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{S}_i(t) &\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\int_0^t {\lambda}_0(u) \cdot \theta_i \, du\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(survival function in terms of the hazard)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\theta_i \cdot {\Lambda}_0(t)\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(factor the constant $\theta_i$ out of the integral)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\theta_i (\lambda t)^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(Weibull cumulative hazard ${\Lambda}_0(t) = (\lambda t)^p$)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\mathopen{}\left(\theta_i^{1/p}\right)\mathclose{}^p (\lambda t)^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(write $\theta_i = \mathopen{}\left(\theta_i^{1/p}\right)\mathclose{}^p$)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\mathopen{}\left(\lambda \cdot t \cdot \theta_i^{1/p}\right)\mathclose{}^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(combine the $p$-th powers)} \\ &= \operatorname{S}_0\mathopen{}\left( t \cdot \theta_i^{1/p} \right)\mathclose{} && \text{(Weibull baseline survival function at $t \cdot \theta_i^{1/p}$)} \end{aligned} \]

Example 8 (A hazard ratio of 4 with Weibull shape 2) Take \(\lambda = 0.05\), \(p = 2\), and \(\theta_i = 4\), with time in weeks. By Theorem 4, the acceleration factor is

\[ \begin{aligned} \theta_i^{1/p} &= 4^{1/2} && \text{(substitute } \theta_i = 4,\ p = 2 \text{)} \\ &= 2 && \text{(square root)} \end{aligned} \]

To check, compute subject \(i\)’s survival at 10 weeks both ways:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left[\operatorname{S}_0(10)\right]\mathclose{}^{\theta_i} &= \mathopen{}\left[{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-(0.05 \cdot 10)^2\right\}\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{}^{4} && \text{(proportional hazards form)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-4 \cdot 0.25\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(} (0.5)^2 = 0.25 \text{; exponent laws)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-1\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(multiply)} \\ \operatorname{S}_0(10 \cdot 2) &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-(0.05 \cdot 20)^2\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(AFT form with acceleration factor 2)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-1\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(} (1)^2 = 1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Both forms give \({\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-1\right\}\mathclose{}} \approx 0.368\): a four-fold hazard means that subject \(i\) ages twice as fast as baseline.

Theorem 5 (The Weibull model is a log-linear model for event times) Suppose subject \(i\) has the Weibull proportional hazards survival function \(\operatorname{S}_i(t) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\theta_i (\lambda t)^p\right\}\mathclose{}}\) of Theorem 4, with \(\theta_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{\eta_i\right\}\mathclose{}}\) for a linear predictor \(\eta_i\). Define \(\alpha \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and \(\sigma \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}1/p\), and let \(W\) have the standard (minimum) Gumbel distribution, \(\mathbb{P}(W > w) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{w}\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Then the log event time \(Y_i \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{T_i\right\}\mathclose{}}\) has the same distribution as

\[ \alpha - \sigma \eta_i + \sigma W. \tag{3}\]

Proof. For any real \(y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(Y_i > y) &= \mathbb{P}(T_i > \text{e}^{y}) && \text{(} \log \text{ is increasing)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\theta_i (\lambda \text{e}^{y})^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(Weibull PH survival function at } \text{e}^{y} \text{)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{\eta_i} (\lambda \text{e}^{y})^p\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(} \theta_i = \text{e}^{\eta_i} \text{)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{\eta_i} \lambda^p \text{e}^{p y}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(power of a product)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{\eta_i} \text{e}^{p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}}} \text{e}^{p y}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(} \lambda^p = \text{e}^{p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}}} \text{)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{\eta_i + p {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}} + p y}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(product rule for exponentials)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{\eta_i + (y - \alpha)/\sigma}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(substitute } p = 1/\sigma,\ {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{\lambda\right\}\mathclose{}} = -\alpha \text{)} \\ &= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{(y - \alpha + \sigma \eta_i)/\sigma}\right\}\mathclose{}} && \text{(common denominator } \sigma \text{)} \\ &= {\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\left(W > \frac{y - \alpha + \sigma \eta_i}{\sigma}\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(Gumbel survival function)} \\ &= {\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\left(\sigma W > y - \alpha + \sigma \eta_i\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(multiply both sides by } \sigma > 0 \text{)} \\ &= {\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\left(\alpha - \sigma \eta_i + \sigma W > y\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(add } \alpha - \sigma \eta_i \text{ to both sides)} \end{aligned} \]

The two random variables have the same survival function, so they have the same distribution.

Example 9 (Median event times from the log-linear form) Take \(\lambda = 0.1\) and \(p = 2\), so \(\alpha = -{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{0.1\right\}\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{10\right\}\mathclose{}} \approx 2.3026\) and \(\sigma = 0.5\). The median of \(W\) solves \({\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\text{e}^{w}\right\}\mathclose{}} = 0.5\), so \(\text{e}^{w} = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and \(w = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{{\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{2\right\}\mathclose{}}\right\}\mathclose{}} \approx -0.3665\). Because \(\alpha - \sigma \eta + \sigma W\) is an increasing function of \(W\), the median of \(Y\) is that function evaluated at the median of \(W\).

For a baseline subject (\(\eta = 0\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{median}(Y) &= \alpha + \sigma \cdot (-0.3665) && \text{(log-linear form at the median of } W \text{)} \\ &= 2.3026 - 0.1833 && \text{(multiply)} \\ &= 2.1193 && \text{(subtract)} \end{aligned} \]

so the median event time is \(\text{e}^{2.1193} \approx 8.33\). Directly, \(\operatorname{S}_0(8.33) = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-(0.833)^2\right\}\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-0.694\right\}\mathclose{}} \approx 0.5\).

For a subject with hazard ratio \(\theta = 4\), so \(\eta = {\operatorname{log}\mathopen{}\left\{4\right\}\mathclose{}} \approx 1.3863\), the median of \(Y\) shifts by \(-\sigma \eta = -0.5 \cdot 1.3863 \approx -0.6931\), so the median event time is multiplied by \(\text{e}^{-0.6931} = 0.5\), giving \(8.33 \cdot 0.5 \approx 4.16\).

By Equation 3, the estimated AFT regression coefficients \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{AFT}}\) produced by R’s survreg() function relate directly to the PH regression coefficients \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{PH}}\) produced by coxph() via the identity \(\hat{\beta}_{\text{AFT}} = -\sigma \hat{\beta}_{\text{PH}}\).

The exponential and Weibull distributions are the only continuous distributions that are simultaneously proportional hazards models and accelerated failure time models. Other parametric families commonly used for survival data (log-logistic, log-normal, and generalized gamma) are AFT families rather than PH families; the log-logistic distribution is additionally a proportional-odds model.

2 Combining left-truncation and interval-censoring

In observational studies and clinical trials, complex observation schemes can introduce both left-truncation (delayed entry) and interval-censoring. Left-truncation occurs when subjects enter the risk set only after surviving past an entry time \(L_{\text{entry}} > 0\). Interval-censoring occurs when the exact event time \(T_i\) is unknown, but is known to fall within an interval \((L_i, R_i]\).

Standard software tools in R make distinct trade-offs between these two observation mechanisms:

coxph does left truncation but not left (or interval) censoring; survreg does interval censoring but not left truncation (or time dependent covariates).

— Terry Therneau, R-help, August 31, 2015

In particular, coxph() constructs risk sets dynamically at each observed failure time \(t_j\) using counting process format (time1 = entry, time2 = exit), enabling seamless handling of left-truncation. However, because partial likelihood relies on ordered exact failure times, standard Cox models cannot easily accommodate interval-censored data.

Conversely, survreg() accommodates interval-censored outcomes via Surv(time1 = L, time2 = R, type = "interval") by maximizing the interval parametric likelihood \(\mathbb{P}(L_i < T_i \le R_i) = \operatorname{F}_0(R_i) - \operatorname{F}_0(L_i)\). However, standard survreg() assumes all subjects enter observation at time 0, and does not condition likelihood terms on surviving past a delayed entry time \(L_{\text{entry}}\).

When a study contains both delayed entry and interval-censored event times, specialized tooling is needed. Among the following R packages, only flexsurv handles both features together; the other two are listed because they cover interval censoring well and are the usual starting points, not because they solve the truncation half:

  • flexsurv: Fits flexible parametric survival models and allows custom likelihood specifications that incorporate both left-truncation conditioning and interval-censored bounds.
  • icenReg: Fits regression models for interval-censored data: Cox proportional-hazards, proportional-odds, and accelerated failure time. Semi-parametric and fully parametric forms are both available, but the fully parametric option covers the AFT models only. It does not itself provide left-truncation conditioning.
  • interval: Fits nonparametric survival curves (NPMLE) for interval-censored data and provides weighted logrank and Wilcoxon-type tests [3]. It is nonparametric throughout, so it supports neither regression nor truncation.
